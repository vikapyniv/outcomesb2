Party round the world

Travel from Trinidad to India and Germany, explore three distinctive carnival traditions and discuss how celebrations reflect history, identity and community.

For those with a party mindset, carnival offers the chance to travel the globe. While places like New Orleans and Rio de Janeiro will always grab the headlines, there are in fact carnivals in over 50 different countries. So, if you’re looking for something different, we have some suggestions - as well as ideas for what’s on offer once the singing and dancing is over.

Discuss after the introduction

  1. Which carnival around the globe would be your all-time first choice, and why?
  2. Why do Rio and New Orleans always grab the headlines while other festivals receive less attention?
  3. What kind of party mindset helps visitors enjoy an unfamiliar celebration?
  4. Can international festivals create common ground between cultures, or can they deepen divisions?
  5. What would you want to explore elsewhere after the singing and dancing was over?
Port of Spain · Trinidad

Caribbean colour and sound

The region’s largest carnival, shaped by resistance, cultural exchange and music.

It’s the largest of all Caribbean carnivals, with 300,000 people on the streets in some of the most incredible costumes you’re ever likely to see. Carnival here dates back to the 19th century, when freed slaves adopted the European festival and made it their own through music and characters like ‘Dame Lorraine’, which caricatured posh people of that time. In the past, calypso musicians sang from the floats in the parade, but these days, you’re more likely to hear soca music and the songs of Lord Shorty blasting out from the sound systems. Soca is a mix of African and East Indian beats which reflects the island’s cultural mix; a mix that is also reflected in the popular carnival dish, doubles, a flatbread sandwich filled with vegetable curry.

Discuss paragraph 1

  1. Why do you think freed slaves adopted a European festival and made it their own?
  2. Can a caricature be both entertaining and a form of sharp social criticism?
  3. How do the African and East Indian beats reveal Trinidad’s cultural mix?
  4. Would music blasting out from sound systems improve the atmosphere for you, or make it overwhelming?
  5. What can traditional food, costumes and floats tell visitors about social divisions and shared identity?

When things have calmed down, Trinidad offers fantastic beaches and wildlife tours as well as cultural sites such as the Temple of the Sea. For the more adventurous, try scuba diving on the stunning reefs.

Discuss paragraph 2

  1. Would you prefer cultural sites or stunning reefs after an intense carnival?
  2. How can a destination tackle the tension between mass tourism and protecting a coral reef?
  3. What makes a sightseeing activity genuinely adventurous?
  4. Should a country be reliant on carnival tourism, or develop attractions elsewhere too?
  5. Which cultural site near your home deserves to grab the headlines?
Panjim · Goa · India

Traditions in motion

A Portuguese idea transformed through Goan and Hindu customs.

Goa hosts one of Asia’s biggest carnivals. The idea of carnival was first introduced by the Portuguese 500 years ago, but it only really took off in the 1960s. Since then, it has grown to a four-day event and now includes many Goan and Hindu traditions. The festival kicks off with a ceremony led by a character called King Momo, who is played by a different local man each year. Among the celebrations that you can enjoy are huge bonfires, sports competitions, mock battles between people throwing eggs and dyed flour, and street theatre with the actors playing jokes on passers-by. Food favourites at the festival are spicy chicken Cafreal and chocolate pancakes with banana, and local people say they consume enough food and drink to feed a village during carnival.

Discuss paragraph 1

  1. Why might Goa’s carnival have taken off in the 1960s rather than earlier?
  2. How does adopting Goan and Hindu traditions change a festival introduced by the Portuguese?
  3. Would you enjoy a mock battle, or would you find jokes on passers-by unfair?
  4. What qualities should a local person have to lead the opening ceremony as King Momo?
  5. Can food help people from different backgrounds find common ground during a celebration?

After all the celebrations, you may want to just lounge on one of the many incredible beaches, but if you still have the energy, visit historic sites like Margao, with its stunning mix of classic Portuguese and Indian architecture.

Discuss paragraph 2

  1. Would you rather lounge on a beach or explore Margao after four days of carnival?
  2. What makes a building or district a genuinely historic site?
  3. Can Portuguese and Indian architecture create a classic style together?
  4. How might tourism change when a carnival becomes a global smash hit?
  5. Which stunning mix of architectural styles have you seen while travelling?
Dieburg · Germany

The fifth season

A centuries-old celebration with protest, parades and jam-filled doughnuts.

People in this tiny town have been celebrating what they call Fünfte Jahreszeit - the fifth season - since the 13th century. Events start in November but culminate in February with, among other things, Old Women’s Day, which celebrates a women’s protest in 1824 and now involves women cutting up the ties of male friends and family! There are fancy dress parades including traditional German bands, and some of the wildest partying of all the many carnival celebrations in Germany. One must-eat treat for partygoers is the Krapfen, delicious jam-filled doughnuts, which help them celebrate long into the night.

Discuss paragraph 1

  1. Why might a festival that dates back to the 13th century still feel relevant?
  2. Does cutting men’s ties keep the meaning of the original women’s protest, or turn it into entertainment?
  3. What should a long festival culminate in to create a memorable ending?
  4. Would traditional bands or modern sound systems create a better carnival atmosphere?
  5. Which sweet food could compete with Krapfen as your all-time favourite festival treat?

After it’s all over and you’ve had all the jam doughnuts you can eat, take a few days exploring the nearby Black Forest with its stunning landscape and beautiful historic villages. For the more active there’s also skiing. For the less active, there’s more cake!

Discuss paragraph 2

  1. Would the Black Forest’s stunning landscape be a welcome change after the crowds?
  2. Does a combination of carnival, historic villages and skiing make a stronger tourist destination?
  3. How can small villages tackle the pressure created by large numbers of visitors?
  4. Would you choose an active holiday or follow the text’s desire for “more cake”?
  5. Which part of this three-country trip has the most interesting plot twist for a traveller?
