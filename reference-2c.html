Talking about the future

Choose between the present simple, present continuous, be going to, will and adjective phrases according to the meaning you want to express.

OverviewPresent simplePresent continuousBe going toWillPredictionsCertainty
Overview

No single future tense

There is no future tense in English. We use different forms depending on what we want to express.

Meaning often overlaps. The distinction between future forms is not always clear or important, and we can sometimes use them interchangeably. The rules below are useful guidelines rather than absolute divisions.
I’m staying in this evening. OR I’m going to stay in this evening.
Both can express a plan, intention or arrangement.
You’ll love Prague. OR You’re going to love Prague.
Both can express a prediction.
Timetables

Present simple

Use the present simple for something fixed as part of a timetable, itinerary or programme.

present simple = fixed scheduled event
My train leaves at nine.
When does the exhibition start?
Arrangements

Present continuous

Use the present continuous for arrangements, or something that is agreed or finalized in some way.

am / is / are + -ing = arranged future
I’m meeting a friend later.
We’re returning to the UK next year.
I’m staying in this evening.
Plans and intentions

Be going to

Use be going to for future plans and intentions that were made before the time of speaking.

am / is / are going to + verb
I’m going to do some exam revision tonight.
We’re not going to say anything to him.
Decisions now

Will

Use will for decisions made at the time of speaking. This includes promises, offers and requests.

will / won’t + verb
I’ll call her now and see what she says.
I’ll help you with that.
Thanks. I’ll pay you back tomorrow.
Evidence or opinion?

Predictions

Present evidence → be going to

For predictions based on direct present evidence - something we can see, hear or otherwise know now - we usually prefer be going to.

Look at the traffic. We’re not going to get there before six.
It’s going to be a nice day.

Personal feeling → will

For predictions based more on personal feeling or opinion, we usually prefer will. We often begin with I think and can put probably between will and the verb.

I think you’ll love Prague. It’s a great city.
I’ll probably regret this tomorrow!
Expected and probable events

Be due to, be likely to and be bound to

Be due to

Use be due to to say when something is scheduled to happen, especially a formal or officially arranged event or situation.

The bus is due to arrive in five minutes.
Entry requirements are due to change next year.

Be likely / unlikely to

Use be likely to for probable events and be unlikely to for improbable events. Add highly or quite to show the degree of likelihood.

We’re unlikely to arrive before midnight.
It’s highly likely the exhibition will be extended.

Be bound to

Use be bound to for something you see as almost certain to happen.

There’s bound to be a delay. There always is.
It’s bound to be sunny in July.
