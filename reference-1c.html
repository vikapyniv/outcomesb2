Adjectives and adverbs

Learn where adjectives and adverbs go, which forms remain unchanged, and how each choice changes the job a word performs in a sentence.

AdjectivesAdjective exceptionsAdverbsAdverb positions
Adjectives

Two main positions

Adjectives describe nouns. They can generally appear in two positions.

1 · Directly before the noun
adjective + noun
The film had a really uplifting ending.
It’s a pretty catchy soundtrack.
2 · After a noun and linking verb
noun + linking verb + adjective
The ending was really uplifting.
The soundtrack is pretty catchy.
Linking verbs

Adjective after the verb

A linking verb connects the subject with a description. It does not describe an action, so it is followed by an adjective.

bebecomegetgofeelgrowlookremainseemsmellstaysoundtasteturn
The young man looks calm.
The colours remain bold.
The explanation sounds convincing.
Watch the position

Some adjectives are restricted

Most adjectives can occur in both positions, but some can only be used before a noun or only after a linking verb.

Position matters:
✓ That’s the main reason.
That reason is main.
✓ Some people were asleep.
There were some asleep people.
Adverbs

How adverbs are formed

Many adverbs are formed by adding -ly to the adjective: slow → slowly, careful → carefully, fortunate → fortunately.

Same adjective and adverb form

Some common adverbs do not add -ly.

fast
hard
early
late
right
daily
yearly
We arrived late for the meeting.
Did I do it right?
He is thinking hard. (not hardly)
Adverb positions

Four common positions

1Before or after verbs

If the verb has an object, the adverb can go after the object.

They slowly wandered from room to room.
She studied the manuscript carefully.
2Before adjectives

An adverb can strengthen or modify an adjective.

His writing is absolutely impossible to read.
3Before other adverbs

One adverb can modify another adverb.

The traffic was moving really slowly.
She speaks incredibly quickly.
4At the start of a sentence or clause

This comments on or expresses an opinion about the whole sentence.

Fortunately, no one was injured in the accident.
Commenting adverb inside a sentence:
I meant to be here earlier, but, stupidly, I got halfway here before I realized I’d left your address at home.
