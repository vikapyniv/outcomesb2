Relative clauses

Use relative clauses to identify people, places and things, add extra information, and comment naturally on a complete idea.

DefiningNon-definingPronounsCommentingCommon mistakesAgreeing
Overview

What is a relative clause?

Sometimes we use a relative clause to give information about someone or something. The relative clause comes immediately after that person or thing.

noun + relative clause
The clause either identifies the noun or adds extra information about it.
Essential information

Defining relative clauses

A defining relative clause gives essential information. The sentence would not make sense, or would not identify the right person or thing, without it.

No comma before a defining relative clause.
He’s the man that / who led our country during the war.
Highgate is the place where Marx is buried.
Bristol is the place where I met my wife.

Leaving out the pronoun

We can omit the relative pronoun when it is the object of the defining clause.

He’s the man (that) I spoke to earlier.
I spoke to the man → the man is the object.
Extra information

Non-defining relative clauses

A non-defining relative clause gives extra, non-essential information. The main sentence remains clear and complete without it.

Always use a relative word and separate the clause from the main sentence with comma(s).
I met my wife in Yonhi-Dong, which is a suburb of Seoul.
Václav Havel, who was also a famous writer, was the first Czech president.
Smith, whose books have been translated into over 30 languages, was born in 1966.
Chosica, where Emmanuel grew up, is a town near Lima.
Choosing the connector

Relative pronouns and adverbs

These forms can be used in both kinds of relative clause, except that, which is only used in defining clauses.

Spoken English

Commenting on a whole clause

We can use a non-defining relative clause with which to comment on, or give an opinion about, the whole previous clause. This is common in spoken English.

We spent a week hiking in the jungle, which was an incredible experience.
We got lost, which meant we missed the start of the performance.
Watch out

Common mistakes

We went on a guided tour round the old town, that was nice.
We went on a guided tour round the old town, which was nice.Do not use that in a non-defining relative clause.
He was born in Mendoza, which it is near the foothills of the Andes.
He was born in Mendoza, which is near the foothills of the Andes.Do not add another pronoun: the relative pronoun replaces it.
She studied at Bologna University which is the oldest university in the world.
She studied at Bologna University, which is the oldest university in the world.Use a comma before a non-defining relative clause.
Developing conversations

Agreeing using synonyms

In conversation, we often repeat an idea with a synonym—a word with a similar meaning—to show that we agree naturally.

A: The houses are lovely round here.
B: Yeah, they’re amazing.
Useful pattern
Yeah / Exactly / Absolutely + synonym

Instead of simply repeating the same adjective, choose a similar one: lovely → amazing, beautiful → stunning, old → historic.
